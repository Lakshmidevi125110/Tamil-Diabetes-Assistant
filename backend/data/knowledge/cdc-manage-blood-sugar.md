# Managing and monitoring blood sugar (CDC)

## Why it matters
Keeping blood sugar in a target range as much as possible helps prevent or delay serious health problems. The page lists general habits that support this: a healthy diet, a healthy weight, regular physical activity, eating at regular times without skipping meals, and tracking blood sugar to learn what makes it rise or fall.

## General eating and drinking points on the page
- Choose lower-calorie foods, and limit saturated fat, sugar and salt.
- Drink water instead of juice or soda, and choose fruit for something sweet.
- Control portions, for example with the plate method.
- Carbohydrate counting is described as an important tool, and the page suggests talking with a healthcare team about carbohydrate goals.
- Alcohol: if drinking, no more than 2 drinks a day for men and 1 drink a day for women.

## Target ranges mentioned (adults, US guidance)
- Before a meal: 80 to 130 mg/dL
- Two hours after the start of a meal: below 180 mg/dL
- Low blood sugar: below 70 mg/dL

Targets can differ depending on age, other health conditions and other factors. The page says to ask the healthcare team which targets are best for the individual.

## Checking blood sugar
- A blood sugar meter (glucometer) or a continuous glucose monitor (CGM) can be used. A CGM uses a sensor under the skin to measure blood sugar every few minutes. People who use one may still need to check with a meter to confirm accuracy.
- Common times to check: on waking before eating or drinking, before meals, two hours after meals, and at bedtime.
- A doctor may advise more frequent checks for people with type 1 diabetes, people with type 2 diabetes who use insulin, or people who often have low blood sugar.

## High blood sugar (hyperglycemia)
Blood sugar that is too high is called hyperglycemia. The page says it can be caused by being sick, being stressed, eating more than usual, or not taking enough insulin.

## Ketones and when to contact a doctor
If a person with diabetes is sick and their blood sugar is 240 mg/dL or above, the page suggests using an over-the-counter ketone test kit. High ketones can be an early sign of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which the page calls a medical emergency needing immediate treatment. A person should contact their doctor if ketones are high.

This is general educational information summarised from the CDC page. It is not personal medical advice, and a person's own targets and schedule should come from their healthcare professional.
